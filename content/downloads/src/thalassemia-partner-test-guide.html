Thalassemia test guide for couples

Many healthy people in India carry a thalassemia gene and never know it. Being a carrier (also called thalassemia minor or trait) is not an illness. It matters for your baby only when both parents carry it. A simple blood test for each of you gives the answer, and the best time to do it is before pregnancy or as early in pregnancy as you can.

The tests, as labs name them

  • CBC (complete blood count) with red cell indices. Look at MCV and MCH. Low values can point to carrier status, or to low iron.
  • HbA2 by HPLC (also written as Hb variant analysis or haemoglobin HPLC). A raised HbA2 is the usual sign of beta thalassemia trait. The report shows the lab's own cut-off.
  • HPLC can also show other haemoglobin types common in parts of India, such as HbE and HbS (sickle). Tell your doctor if either appears.
  • If results are unclear, the doctor may add iron studies (serum ferritin) or a DNA test.

Who tests, and when

  • One partner gets tested first, often the woman at her first pregnancy visit.
  • If she is a carrier, or her result is unclear, her husband gets tested quickly. Do not wait.
  • Many doctors suggest both partners test together. It saves time.
  • If you are related by blood (for example, cousins), testing both partners matters even more, because you are more likely to share the same gene.
  • Carry both reports to every visit.

If you are both carriers

You have choices, and time matters. Tests can check whether the baby has thalassemia major. CVS is usually done around 10 to 13 weeks, and amniocentesis from about 15 weeks. A specialist explains what each involves, so you can decide what is right for your family.

Iron and carriers

Carriers can look mildly anaemic on a CBC even with enough iron. Do not start or stop iron tablets on your own. Your doctor decides based on your iron levels. In pregnancy, iron is often still needed.
